Research cycle, study designs, sampling, validity, reporting, ethics

Research Methods & Study Designs

TOS domain · Research

Every study begins with a question, and the question decides the design. Get the direction of sampling right (exposure first or outcome first) and most design items become straightforward.

🌀The research cycle

🔮
Question first, data second
Fix the question and the methods before collecting anything. Letting convenient data shape the question risks measuring the wrong variables and missing the ones you need.
🔮
What the literature review does
It refines the problem, exposes knowledge gaps, and flags likely confounders and effect modifiers. It does not prove causation or replace a design.

🗺️Finding the design

⚡
"Retrospective" is not a design
A historical cohort still moves from exposure to outcome using old records. Ask what the participants were selected on, not when the data were collected.

🏹Sampling

Non-probability methods (convenience, purposive, quota, snowball, volunteer) are quicker but invite selection bias. In clinical studies, enrolling every consecutive eligible patient is the strongest non-random option, better than volunteers or physician referrals. Multistage sampling, used in national surveys, chains clusters, sub-clusters, and individuals.

An uncontrolled pre-post study cannot separate the intervention from time trends or regression to the mean. Even a wait-list control group strengthens internal validity.

🗝️Beyond the source notes

🗝️
Pragmatic vs explanatory trials
Explanatory trials test whether a treatment can work under ideal conditions, with strict eligibility and close monitoring. Pragmatic trials test whether it works in routine care, with broad eligibility and usual clinicians. The PRECIS-2 tool helps place a trial on this spectrum and signals how far results generalize.
🗝️
Registering a trial before enrollment
Journals following ICMJE policy publish trials only if they were registered before the first participant enrolled, for example in ClinicalTrials.gov or a WHO registry. Registration fixes the primary outcome in advance, which makes selective outcome reporting easier to detect.

🦉Check yourself

🦉 Answer each one aloud before you reveal it
1.A health office randomly assigns 24 barangays, not individual residents, to a hygiene program or usual services. What design is this?
A cluster randomized trial: intact groups are the unit of randomization.
2.Investigators recruit patients with and without hip fracture, then ask about past sedative use. Which design, and which measure?
Case-control, since groups were defined by outcome. It reports an odds ratio.
3.A librarian searches a database that indexes drug literature with Emtree. Which database is it? Which one uses MeSH?
Embase uses Emtree; MEDLINE (PubMed) uses MeSH.
4.A team pools 15 studies on the accuracy of a rapid dengue test. Which reporting guideline fits their paper?
PRISMA-DTA, for systematic reviews of diagnostic accuracy studies.
5.A study develops and validates a score predicting ICU admission in pneumonia. Which reporting guideline applies?
TRIPOD, for multivariable prediction models.
6.Why must sampling, measurements, and the analysis plan be fixed before data are collected?
They define how the hypothesis will be tested. Choosing them after seeing the data invites bias.
7.A study of poor adherence to antihypertensives first blames cost, but the literature review also points to health beliefs and side effects. What is the review contributing?
A basis for deciding which variables and relationships (including possible confounders) to test formally.
8.Analysis, interpretation, and conclusions are complete. What finishes the research cycle?
Disseminating the findings through appropriate scientific channels.
9.A funder must choose between a pre-post study with no controls and a similar study with a wait-list control. Which has better internal validity, and why?
The wait-list control design, because it provides a comparison group.
10.Reviewers can fund only one change to a cohort protocol to strengthen causal claims. Which change most directly secures temporality?
Measuring exposure status before the outcome occurs.

Describing data, the normal curve, confidence intervals, testing, test choice

Biostatistics & Hypothesis Testing

TOS domain · Biostatistics

Biostatistics asks two things: how precise is our estimate, and how surprised should we be by the result if nothing were going on? Precision comes from the standard error; surprise comes from the p-value.

🕯️Describing data

The median is the safer summary for skewed data. Spread is described by the range, interquartile range, variance, and standard deviation. The coefficient of variation (SD ÷ mean × 100) compares spread across different units.

🌙The normal curve

z-score
(x − mean) / SD
How many SDs a value lies from the mean
Standard error of the mean
SEM = SD / √n
Shrinks as n grows; the SD does not
95% confidence interval
mean ± 1.96 × SEM
99% CI uses 2.58 × SEM, so it is wider
📜
A 95% CI from scratch
n = 100, mean = 80, SD = 10 → SEM = 10/√100 = 1 → 95% CI = 80 ± 1.96 = 78.04 to 81.96
⚡
SD or SEM?
Using ±1.96 SD gives the range of individual values, not the precision of the mean. For a confidence interval, always divide by √n first.

⚖️Hypothesis testing

Truth: no effect (H₀ true)
Truth: real effect (H₀ false)
Reject H₀
Type Ifalse positive · α
Powercorrect · 1 − β
Keep H₀
Correct1 − α
Type IIfalse negative · β
  • p-value: the chance of results at least this extreme if H₀ were true, not the chance that H₀ is true.
  • Power: usually set at 80%; rises with sample size, effect size, α, and lower variability.
  • Reading a CI: not significant if a difference CI includes 0, or a ratio CI (RR, OR, HR) includes 1.
🔮
Chance errors, not bias
α and β describe random, inferential errors. Confounding and measurement bias are systematic errors that no significance level can detect. A small study mainly risks a Type II error: a real effect goes unnoticed.

Correlation r runs from −1 to +1; r² is the share of variance explained. Correlation alone never proves causation.

🗝️Beyond the source notes

🗝️
The multiple-comparisons problem
Test 20 independent outcomes at α = 0.05 and you should expect about one "significant" result by chance alone. Prespecifying a primary outcome, or adjusting with a method such as Bonferroni (α divided by the number of tests), keeps the false-positive rate in check. This is why unplanned subgroup findings deserve skepticism.
🗝️
Regression to the mean
People selected because of an extreme first measurement (very high BP, a bad pain day) tend to score closer to average when re-measured, with no treatment at all. Only a comparison group separates this drift from a true treatment effect.

🦉Check yourself

🦉 Answer each one aloud before you reveal it
1.A trial concludes a drug lowers stroke risk, but in truth the drug has no effect. Which error was made?
A Type I error: rejecting a true null hypothesis (a false positive).
2.How does a Type II error differ from a Type I error?
A Type II error misses a real effect (fails to reject a false null); a Type I error finds an effect that is not there.
3.Define α in one sentence.
The probability of rejecting the null hypothesis when it is actually true.
4.Why does an undersized study tend to produce Type II errors?
Small samples have low power, so a real effect often fails to reach significance.
5."If the effect we designed for truly exists, how likely are we to detect it?" Which quantity is this?
Power, 1 − β.
6.Mean hemoglobin is 13.0 g/dL in 36 patients, SD 1.8. What is the approximate 95% CI?
SEM = 1.8/6 = 0.3; 13.0 ± 1.96 × 0.3 = 12.4 to 13.6 g/dL.
7.A study reports p = 0.04. State what this does and does not mean.
If H₀ were true, results this extreme would occur about 4% of the time. It is not a 4% chance that H₀ is true.
8.An OR of 1.6 has a 95% CI of 0.8 to 3.1. Significant or not?
Not significant: the ratio interval includes 1.
9.Which test compares mean cholesterol across four diet groups?
One-way ANOVA (Kruskal-Wallis if the data are skewed).
10.Household income data have a long right tail. Which is larger, the mean or the median, and which should be reported?
The mean is larger; report the median.

Epidemiologic Measures

TOS domain · Biostatistics / Epidemiology

Most calculation items are one of three moves: find a risk, compare two risks by ratio or by difference, or turn a difference into "how many people." Set up the 2×2 table first and the rest follows.

🪣How often: frequency measures

Cumulative incidence (risk)
new cases / population at risk
Over a stated period; the attack rate in outbreaks
Incidence density (rate)

Each person contributes time until the event, loss, or study end
Prevalence

A snapshot
Case fatality rate
deaths / cases × 100
Severity, not a true rate
📜
Counting person-time
200 people, 3-year study: 10 events at 1.5 years, 20 lost at 1 year, 170 complete → 15 + 20 + 510 = 545 person-years → 10/545 = 18.3 per 1,000 person-years
⚡
A better treatment can raise prevalence
A drug that prolongs life without curing keeps people in the tub longer, so prevalence rises even though incidence is unchanged.

🔮

An RRR looks impressive even when the baseline risk is tiny. ARR and NNT tell patients how much difference to expect.

⚖️Fair comparisons: standardization

  • Direct: apply each group's age-specific rates to one standard population to get age-adjusted rates.
  • Indirect: apply standard rates to the study group's age structure to get expected deaths.
  • SMR = observed ÷ expected deaths, where expected = Σ (people in each age group × standard rate).
  • An SMR below 1 in workers often reflects the healthy worker effect.

🗝️Beyond the source notes

🗝️
Secondary attack rate
In outbreaks, the secondary attack rate is the number of new cases among contacts divided by the number of susceptible contacts, excluding the primary case. It measures how readily an infection spreads within households or close groups, and it is how contact-tracing data are summarized.
🗝️
Hazard ratio
Cox regression reports a hazard ratio: the ratio of event rates at any moment during follow-up. It is read like an RR (1 = no difference, 0.7 = 30% lower hazard), but it uses the timing of events, not only whether they happened.

🦉Check yourself

🦉 Answer each one aloud before you reveal it
1.Cervical cancer mortality falls from 0.9% to 0.5% among screened women. How many must be screened to prevent one death?
ARR = 0.4% → NNS = 1/0.004 = 250.
2.Pertussis struck 60 of 3,000 vaccinated and 200 of 2,000 unvaccinated children. What is vaccine effectiveness?
Attack rates 2% and 10% → VE = (10 − 2)/10 = 80%.
3.Hospitalization occurred in 80 of 1,000 on usual care and 50 of 1,000 on a new drug. What is the NNT?
ARR = 8% − 5% = 3% → 1/0.03 = 33.3, rounded up to 34.
4.Hearing loss developed in 150 of 1,000 noise-exposed and 50 of 1,000 unexposed workers. What is the attributable risk?
15% − 5% = 10%.
5.A drug causes hypokalemia in 40 of 400 patients versus 16 of 400 on the comparator. What is the NNH?
ARI = 10% − 4% = 6% → 1/0.06 = 16.7, rounded up to 17.
6.300 people followed 2 years: 30 fall ill at an average of 1 year, 30 drop out at 1 year, 240 finish disease-free. Incidence density per 1,000 person-years?
Person-years = 30 + 30 + 480 = 540 → 30/540 = 55.6 per 1,000 person-years.
7.Observed deaths = 72. Expected: 400 × 0.05, 500 × 0.08, and 300 × 0.10. What is the SMR?
Expected = 20 + 40 + 30 = 90 → SMR = 72/90 = 0.80.
8.Disease incidence is 20% in exposed and 5% in unexposed workers. What fraction of disease in the exposed is due to exposure?
(20 − 5)/20 = 75%.
9.Cases: 60 exposed, 40 unexposed. Controls: 40 exposed, 60 unexposed. What is the odds ratio?
(60 × 60)/(40 × 40) = 2.25.
10.Asthma developed in 90 of 300 exposed and 50 of 500 unexposed children. What is the relative risk?
0.30/0.10 = 3.0.

Diagnostic & Screening Tests

TOS domain · Biostatistics / EBM

A test result only changes what you believe; it never replaces the belief you started with. Sensitivity and specificity belong to the test. Predictive values belong to the test in a particular population.

⚡
Screening a rare disease
With very low prevalence, even a small false-positive rate applied to a huge healthy group swamps the true positives: low PPV, extra confirmatory tests, cost, and anxiety. Screening high-risk groups raises prevalence and PPV.

⏳Judging a screening program

Screening biasWhat happens
Lead-time biasEarlier detection, same date of death
Length-time biasScreening finds slow-growing disease more often

🔮
The fair yardstick
Judge screening by disease-specific mortality, not survival from diagnosis. Wilson-Jungner criteria ask for an important problem, a detectable latent stage, an acceptable test, effective treatment, and reasonable cost.

🗝️Beyond the source notes

🗝️
Quick bedside rule for likelihood ratios
For pretest probabilities between about 10% and 90%, an LR of 2, 5, or 10 raises probability by roughly 15, 30, or 45 percentage points; an LR of 0.5, 0.2, or 0.1 lowers it by the same amounts (McGee). It is a fast way to judge whether a test result would change management.
🗝️
Spectrum bias
Accuracy studies that compare obvious cases with healthy volunteers overstate sensitivity and specificity. Real patients with milder or look-alike disease are harder to classify, which is why STARD asks for consecutive, clinically suspected patients.

🦉 Answer each one aloud before you reveal it
1.In an accuracy study of 1,200 patients, the reference standard confirms disease in 300. What is the prevalence?
300/1,200 = 25%.
2.TP 180, FP 60, FN 20, TN 540. What is the sensitivity?
180/(180 + 20) = 90%.

8.Two populations show the same sensitivity, specificity, and LRs but very different PPVs. What do you check first?
Disease prevalence in each population.
9.A national program screens for a 1-in-2,000 disease with a moderately specific test. What is the main downside?
Many false positives among healthy people: low PPV and a burden of confirmatory tests and referrals.
10.Why does restricting screening to people with known risk factors raise PPV?
It raises prevalence among those tested, so true positives rise relative to false positives.

Bias, Confounding & Causation

TOS domain · Research / EBM

Before believing any association, rule out three rivals in order: chance, bias, and confounding. Only then weigh causation.

🏹Two kinds of error

⚗️Where bias enters a trial

⚡
Concealment is not blinding
Allocation concealment works before assignment and prevents selection bias. Blinding works after assignment and prevents performance and detection bias.
⚡
Intention-to-treat vs per-protocol
ITT keeps everyone in their randomized group and preserves balance. Per-protocol drops non-adherent participants and can overstate benefit when dropouts relate to side effects or poor response.

🌿Confounding

A confounder is linked to the exposure, independently raises the outcome risk, and is not on the causal path. The classic clue: the crude association weakens or vanishes after adjustment. In confounding by indication, sicker patients get the treatment, making it look harmful.

StageHow to control confounding
DesignRandomization (known and unknown factors), restriction, matching
AnalysisStratification, multivariable regression, standardization, propensity scores
⚡
What does not fix confounding
A bigger sample improves precision only. Blinding assessors addresses detection bias. Effect modification is a real difference between strata: report it, do not adjust it away.

⛓️Is it causal?

Temporalitycause before effect (essential)Strengthlarge RR or ORConsistencyreplicatedDose-responsemore exposure, more diseasePlausibilityfits biologyCoherencefits natural historyExperimentremoval reduces diseaseAnalogysimilar causes, similar effectsSpecificityone cause, one effect (weakest)

These are the Bradford Hill criteria. Only temporality is required; the rest add weight.

🗝️Beyond the source notes

🗝️
Collider bias
A collider is a variable caused by both the exposure and the outcome, such as hospital admission caused by two different diseases. Restricting a study to hospitalized patients (conditioning on the collider) can create an association between two diseases that are unrelated in the community. Adjusting for a collider adds bias rather than removing it.
🗝️
Drawing a causal diagram first
Directed acyclic graphs (DAGs), like the three above, make assumptions explicit and show which variables to adjust for: confounders yes, mediators and colliders no. They are now common in the methods sections of observational studies.

Maternal death has a strict definition
Death during pregnancy or within 42 days of its end, from causes related to or aggravated by the pregnancy or its management. Accidental and incidental causes are excluded, so "pregnancy-associated" deaths are a broader count.

🗝️Beyond the source notes

🗝️
The demographic dividend
When fertility falls, a country briefly has many working-age adults relative to children and elderly people. If those adults are healthy, educated, and employed, the low dependency ratio can speed economic growth. The window closes as the large cohorts age, so investment in health and education has to come early.
🗝️
The Philippines is below replacement fertility
The 2022 NDHS reported a total fertility rate of 1.9 children per woman, down from 2.7 in 2017 and below the replacement level of about 2.1. Expect the national pyramid to keep narrowing at the base in coming censuses.

🦉Check yourself

🦉 Answer each one aloud before you reveal it
1.Which numerator and denominator produce the crude birth rate?
All live births in the year over the total midyear population, × 1,000.
2.An item offers the midyear infant population as the IMR denominator. Why is that wrong?
The IMR uses live births in the same year as its denominator.
3.Maternal deaths are divided by women aged 15–49. Is this the maternal mortality ratio?
No. That is the maternal mortality rate; the ratio uses live births.
4.A town of 80,000 has 20,000 women aged 15–49 and 1,400 live births. What is the GFR?
1,400/20,000 × 1,000 = 70 per 1,000 women aged 15–49.
5.A province with 250,000 women aged 15–49 recorded 9 maternal deaths and 30,000 live births. What is the MMR?
9/30,000 × 100,000 = 30 per 100,000 live births.
6.A district had 4,000 live births and 28 infant deaths (12 of them neonatal). What is the IMR?
28/4,000 × 1,000 = 7.0 per 1,000 live births.
7.A city of 200,000 (65,000 women aged 15–49) recorded 3,600 live births. What is the CBR?
3,600/200,000 × 1,000 = 18 per 1,000.
8.A province of 150,000 (90,000 adults) recorded 900 deaths. What is the CDR?
900/150,000 × 1,000 = 6.0 per 1,000. The adult count is a distractor.
9.A pyramid has a wide base that narrows steadily upward, with no major migration. What does it suggest?
High fertility and rapid growth, with a high youth dependency ratio.
10.A pyramid shows fewer children than adults and a bulge in middle age, with stable mortality. What history fits?
A fall in fertility after an earlier baby boom.

Evidence-Based Medicine

TOS domain · Evidence-Based Medicine

Evidence-based medicine is a way of deciding, not a ranking of papers. The best available evidence is weighed with clinical expertise and what the patient values.

🌀The five steps

🏰Which evidence is strongest?

  • Cohort below RCT: without randomization, groups may differ in prognosis despite adjustment.
  • Case series: no comparison group, so outcomes cannot be credited to the treatment.
  • Expert opinion: no systematic control of bias.

🌳Reading a meta-analysis

FeatureWhat to know
HeterogeneityCochran Q and I²: about 25% low, 50% moderate, 75% high

⚡
Precise does not mean right
Pooling shrinks random error but cannot remove bias built into the included studies. Garbage in, garbage out.

PrincipleWhy it matters

🗝️Beyond the source notes

🗝️
Appraising a clinical practice guideline
Guidelines vary in quality. The AGREE II instrument rates them across six domains, including rigor of development, editorial independence, and stakeholder involvement. A guideline built on a systematic review, with conflicts of interest managed, deserves more trust than a consensus document.
🗝️
Minimal clinically important difference
The MCID is the smallest change in an outcome that patients would notice as meaningful. A statistically significant 2-point improvement on a pain scale may still fall short of an MCID of about 10 to 20 points on a 100-point scale, so the result would not matter to patients.

🦉Check yourself

🦉 Answer each one aloud before you reveal it
1.A panel asks whether a drug reduces mortality. All studies are sound and answer the same PICO. Which evidence should carry the most weight?
A systematic review of several high-quality RCTs of the same comparison.
2.A large, well-adjusted prospective cohort still ranks below an RCT for therapy. Why?
Non-randomized groups may still differ in prognosis, including unmeasured confounding and confounding by indication.
3.Why can a case series not show that a treatment works?
It has no comparison group, so outcomes cannot be attributed to the treatment.
4.A meta-analysis gives a very narrow CI, yet its trials lacked allocation concealment. How can it be precise but biased?
Pooling reduces random error but cannot remove systematic bias in the included studies.
5.Why does expert opinion sit at the base of the evidence pyramid?
It lacks the systematic control of bias that formal study designs provide.
6.Drug X has an NNT of 10 and Drug Y an NNT of 50 for the same outcome and period, with similar safety. Which is more efficient?
Drug X: fewer patients must be treated per event prevented.
7.A drug has an NNT of 20 to prevent one stroke and an NNH of 25 for one major bleed. Is it favored because 20 is less than 25?
No. The severity and reversibility of stroke versus bleeding must be weighed, along with patient values.
8.Two trials both report a 30% RRR, but only one also gives the ARR and NNT. Why is that one more useful clinically?
Absolute measures show the expected benefit at the observed baseline risk.
9.A trial reports p < 0.001 with an NNT of 250. How large is the practical benefit?
Small: the evidence is strong but the absolute benefit is modest.
10."In adults with sepsis, does early albumin, compared with saline, reduce 28-day mortality?" Name the I and the C.
I = early albumin; C = saline.

Quality Assurance & Patient Safety

TOS domain · Quality Assurance

Quality work asks what we have (structure), what we do (process), and what happens to patients (outcome), then improves the system in small, measured steps rather than looking for someone to blame.

🏛️Donabedian: the three lenses

🏢 StructureResources and setting: staff, equipment, facilities, qualifications
🩺 ProcessWhat providers do: hand hygiene, guideline-concordant care, waiting times
❤️ OutcomeChange in patient health: mortality, infections, function, complications
⚡
Outcomes need risk adjustment
Outcomes are the most direct measure of quality but also the most affected by case mix. Compare hospitals on risk-adjusted rates, and track all three lenses together rather than satisfaction alone.
SafeTimelyEffectiveEfficientEquitablePatient-centered

These six IOM dimensions (STEEEP) define good care. Quality assurance checks performance against standards; continuous quality improvement keeps raising the standard across the whole system.

♾️Improving: the PDSA cycle

🔮
One framework for everyone
A validated framework used organization-wide gives comparable, sustainable results. Each department improvising its own process does not.

🗝️Beyond the source notes

🗝️
Lean and Six Sigma
Lean, from the Toyota Production System, removes waste such as waiting, rework, and unnecessary movement, so patients flow through care faster. Six Sigma reduces variation using data, following the DMAIC steps: define, measure, analyze, improve, control. Many hospitals combine them as "Lean Six Sigma."
🗝️
High-reliability organizations
Aviation and nuclear power run hazardous operations with very few accidents. Hospitals borrow their habits: preoccupation with failure, reluctance to simplify explanations, attention to frontline operations, resilience, and deference to whoever has the most expertise, regardless of rank.

🌿Commonly confused pairs

🦉 Answer each one aloud before you reveal it
1.Case-control vs cohort: what defines the groups, and which measure does each give?
Case-control starts from the outcome (OR); a cohort starts from exposure (RR).
2.Type I vs Type II error?
Type I is a false positive (α); Type II is a false negative (β). Power = 1 − β.
3.Which improves with a bigger sample: precision or validity?
Precision only.
4.Which test values move with prevalence?
PPV and NPV. Sensitivity and specificity stay with the test.
5.Allocation concealment vs blinding: which bias does each prevent?
Concealment prevents selection bias; blinding prevents performance and detection bias.
6.Selective outcome reporting vs publication bias?
One is within a published study; the other is about whole studies going unpublished.
7.ITT vs per-protocol: which preserves randomization?
ITT. Per-protocol may overstate benefit.
8.Confounder vs effect modifier: what do you do with each?
Control a confounder; report an effect modifier by stratum.
9.RCA vs FMEA: which looks backward and which looks ahead?
RCA investigates an event after it happens; FMEA anticipates failures before they occur.
10.A proposed program maps risk, picks evidence-based measures, and expands "if cases drop." Which link of the closed loop is missing?
Iterative testing (PDSA) guided by predefined indicators that feed redesign.

Wrong options usually drop one link: no formal risk analysis, no evidence appraisal, no iterative testing, or only "expand if trends improve" with no predefined indicators.
